📚 A Brief History of Hot Plastic

Sixty-plus years of whirr-clunk-hiss, compressed into one very long scrolling page, as is tradition.

YEARWHAT HAPPENED
1950sInventor John H. "Tike" Miller of Quincy, Illinois can't find a replacement figure for his broken nativity set — so he starts molding his own, and dreams up a free-standing machine that molds plastic figures on demand.
1962Mold-A-Rama machines debut, wowing crowds around the Seattle World's Fair era. Miller licenses the technology to Automatic Retailers of America (later Aramark), who put machines everywhere.
1964The New York World's Fair makes Mold-A-Rama a national sensation. Sinclair Oil's "Dinoland" dinosaur molds become the stuff of legend (and of every kid's sock drawer).
1968-72ARA gets out of the hot plastic business and sells off the machines. Every machine that exists today was built before this — nobody has manufactured a new Mold-A-Rama since the 1960s.
1971Bill Jones buys a dozen machines (10 at Brookfield Zoo, 2 at the Museum of Science & Industry). His family company — today's Mold-A-Rama® Inc. of Brookfield, IL — eventually acquires the trademark itself and keeps the Midwest fleet humming.
late '60s+Meanwhile in the South: appliance-store owner Eldin Irwin buys up ARA machines and builds the fleet now run by Unique Souvenirs Inc. under the Mold-A-Matic™ brand — the Florida circuit fans know today.
2015A count finds 124 machines operating in eight states. Not bad for 50-year-old hardware.
TodayMachines keep popping up in new venues — record shops, libraries, even a Vegas pinball hall. Price is up to $3–$5, the smell is exactly the same, and you can still find them.
⚙ How the machine works (the good part)
  1. You pay and push the button. The show begins.
  2. Two halves of an aluminum mold clamp together under the bubble dome.
  3. Molten polyethylene (~250°F) squirts up into the mold.
  4. A blast of air hollows out the figure — that's why your souvenir is light and thin-walled.
  5. Coolant chills the mold, the halves part with a thunk, and a scraper shoves your still-warm figure into the chute.
  6. Total time: under a minute. Total smell: unforgettable.
Pro tip from the Tracker crowd: color changeovers happen gradually, so a machine switching from red to yellow makes rare ORANGE figures for a while. Collectors go nuts for these.
Sources: fan lore, museum plaques, and staring through the bubble dome for hours.
